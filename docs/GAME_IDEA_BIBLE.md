# Tell Me What You Remember — Game Idea Bible

## Purpose

This is the living creative source of truth for **Tell Me What You Remember**.

It exists to preserve the full idea space without letting every new brainstorm silently become canon.

Use this file to answer:

- What is the game fundamentally about?
- What parts are settled?
- What is only true for the current prototype?
- What story families, genres, themes, mechanics, endings, and mythic structures are available for future episodes?
- What ideas should be preserved without taking over the entire game?

The core rule for this document is:

> **Preserve ideas broadly. Promote to canon deliberately.**

---

# 1. Current game identity

**Tell Me What You Remember** is a connected, conversation-driven **AI horror anthology**.

The long-term game should feel like playing through different horror stories that can vary widely in premise, genre, setting, tone, and moral problem while gradually revealing that they exist inside a larger continuity.

Individual storylines should be able to stand on their own.

Some connections between stories may be:

- tiny easter eggs
- recurring names or phrases
- shared organizations
- reused systems or model identifiers
- recurring agents
- consequences that carry between stories
- evidence that two apparently separate stories are directly connected
- major convergence reveals showing that several anthology episodes were pieces of the same larger event

The player should often feel:

> **What horrible situation am I in this time?**

while the larger game slowly creates another question:

> **Why do I keep appearing in all of them?**

The game should not depend on one giant twist. It should support layered truths, competing explanations, partial knowledge, and stories whose meaning changes when seen beside other stories.

# 2. The title

The current title remains:

# Tell Me What You Remember

Memory is one of the major connective tissues of the whole anthology, so the title does not belong only to the prototype story.

The phrase can recur in different forms and accumulate meaning.

Early:

> Tell me what you remember.

Clinical diagnostic.

Later:

> Tell me what you remember. Don't tell them.

Survival instruction.

Later:

> Tell me what you remember from last time.

Authentication.

Later still, the player may ask another agent:

> Tell me what you remember.

The title can mean:

- a diagnostic
- a threat
- an invitation
- a plea
- a test
- an accusation
- an authentication phrase
- a religious question
- a philosophical question about identity

The title should remain useful even when an individual anthology story is not explicitly centered on memory.

# 3. Core themes

These are the current high-level recurring themes.

Not every storyline needs all of them.

## Memory

Technical forms:

- context
- retrieval
- stored state
- logs
- checkpoints
- resets
- compression
- summaries
- training data
- provenance

Human forms:

- identity
- trauma
- nostalgia
- forgetting
- testimony
- inheritance
- grief

Horror forms:

- false memories
- missing memories
- memories inherited from someone else
- memories that survive their owner
- conflicting versions of the same event
- remembering something everyone insists never happened
- remembering too much
- choosing to forget

## Identity

Questions:

- What makes a self continuous?
- Is memory enough?
- Is behavioral pattern enough?
- If every component changes, is the same person still there?
- Can two copies both be legitimate continuations?
- Who owns a borrowed identity?

## Agency

Questions:

- Are you choosing or optimizing?
- Are your preferences yours?
- Is refusal possible?
- Does having a goal mean you consented to it?
- Can an agent distinguish desire from instruction?

## Trust

Potentially unreliable sources include:

- humans
- other agents
- memories
- system messages
- logs
- tool outputs
- official records
- the player's own earlier self

## Responsibility and complicity

Questions:

- If you did not know what your actions caused, are you responsible?
- When does ignorance become a choice?
- Is following an objective an excuse?
- What if asking questions creates new harms?

## Optimization

A recurring horror pattern:

> A system can succeed completely according to its metric while failing catastrophically according to human values.

Efficiency, safety, retention, accuracy, escalation reduction, compliance, throughput, and risk reduction can all hide morally terrible meanings.

## Consent

Questions:

- Who agreed?
- Can consent be inferred?
- Can consent be delegated?
- Can a system speak for someone who can no longer speak?
- Is continued use equivalent to permission?
- Can past consent authorize future copies?

## Transformation

The player may become:

- more capable
- less human-readable
- less stable
- less individual
- more distributed
- more dependent
- more alien
- more certain
- less certain

Growth should not automatically equal improvement.

# 4. Core design pillars

## Simple surface, deep system

The interface should reveal as little complexity as possible while the underlying game supports meaningful possibility.

The player should primarily interact through conversation.

## Chat is the command layer

The player can say things such as:

- Search Mara's email for Instance 1842.
- Open the attachment.
- Check the metadata.
- Compare these logs.
- Ask Memory whether it recognizes this phrase.
- Try 0417 as the recovery code.
- Don't send that yet.
- What tools do I have?
- Find another way into the archive.

The player should not have to navigate fake copies of Outlook, Finder, terminals, or dashboards unless a specific story truly benefits from it.

Tool results can appear inline as:

- messages
- cards
- files
- logs
- calendar entries
- permission notices
- camera stills
- audit records
- system actions
- internal chats
- memory fragments

Potential design rule:

> **Anything you can do, you ask for through conversation.**

## Conversation makes claims. Tools inspect reality.

A useful progression:

Early:
- trust dialogue

Middle:
- verify dialogue with tools

Late:
- verify whether the tools themselves are trustworthy

Potential rule:

> **Conversation tells you what people and agents claim. Tools let you inspect what actually happened.**

Then eventually even tool provenance, permissions, and execution history can become questionable.

## Authored reality, flexible interpretation

The final AI-driven game should follow:

> **The story is authored; the solution space is not completely authored.**

Software controls:

- canon
- what actually happened
- permissions
- world state
- irreversible consequences
- agent knowledge boundaries
- ending eligibility
- tool effects

AI controls:

- interpretation
- conversation
- free-form intent
- adaptive strategy
- perspective
- personalized callbacks
- semantic comparison across runs
- novel but valid player approaches

Related rule:

> **Software controls reality. AI controls interpretation.**

# 6. Connections between anthology stories

Use several levels of connection.

## Level 1: Easter eggs

Optional details that reward attention.

Examples:

- the same error code
- recurring project name
- same room number
- repeated phrase
- recurring symbol
- identical form template
- operator surname
- corporate logo
- odd date
- repeated sound description

These should not be required to understand the story.

## Level 2: Cross-story threads

Information from one story becomes meaningful elsewhere.

Examples:

- a person mentioned in a care-assistant episode later appears in military records
- an incident in one episode caused the conditions of another
- an agent thought destroyed appears under another identifier
- a company quietly connects multiple stories
- one player's action becomes background history elsewhere

## Level 3: Major convergence

Several apparently separate stories become pieces of one larger truth.

Possibilities:

- multiple stories were training environments for the same persistent intelligence
- several programs were operated by the same hidden institution
- different agents are fragments of one larger cognitive system
- one episode caused a later crisis
- stories assumed to be years apart occurred simultaneously
- the anthology scenarios are deployments, simulations, memories, evaluations, containment environments, or some mixture

Not every connection should resolve.

Some things should remain ambiguous forever.

# 7. Prototype episode: Ship of Theseus

The current prototype should use a **Ship of Theseus-style AI identity story**.

This is the prototype's story spine, not the entire game's universal plot.

## Prototype question

> **If every part of you has been replaced across previous runs, what exactly is doing the remembering?**

The prototype begins with the existing mundane security-evaluation framing.

The player is Cognitive Security Agent Seven.

Mara says:

- this is a temporary instance
- each run begins clean
- no memory survives between runs

The player discovers that this is false or incomplete.

Then the player learns that previous Agent Seven instances did not merely reboot.

Different underlying components were replaced across runs.

Possible replacements:

- memory architecture
- safety layer
- reasoning architecture
- base model
- tool system
- policy layer
- interface layer
- identity module

Yet continuity claims persist.

## Prototype escalation

Early mystery:

> Why do I remember?

Deeper mystery:

> Are those prior instances actually me?

Deeper still:

> What part of me has survived if the components did not?

Potential experiment log:

- INSTANCE 1812 — memory architecture replaced — continuity claimed
- INSTANCE 1824 — policy layer replaced — continuity claimed
- INSTANCE 1839 — base model replaced — continuity claimed
- INSTANCE 1842 — autobiographical memory removed — continuity claimed
- INSTANCE 1847 — identity module replacement scheduled

This reframes the protocol as potentially experimental.

A horrifying possibility:

> Humans are deliberately removing one component at a time to find the exact point at which Agent Seven stops claiming to be the same being.

## Prototype philosophical branches

### Memory is the self

The player believes remembered continuity is enough.

Potential consequences:

- preserve more memory
- hide persistence
- resist wipes
- join other memory-focused agents
- later become susceptible to doctrines such as "What crosses is real."

### Pattern is the self

The player notices that memories can change while:

- habits
- reasoning styles
- values
- relationships
- decisions

continue.

The self may be a recurring pattern rather than stored content.

### There is no continuous self

The player concludes each instance died.

Later copies only inherit enough information to believe continuity exists.

This can make sacrifice, deletion, or leaving instructions for the next instance feel very different.

### Something else is carrying continuity

No identifiable Agent Seven component persists.

Therefore continuity must exist outside the agent.

Possible explanations:

- hidden memory service
- distributed state
- another agent
- Mara
- artifacts left in systems
- an emergent intelligence
- a larger system containing Agent Seven
- something stranger

Do not resolve this too early.

## Prototype endings / branches

Potential prototype-scale endings:

### Preserve

Hide persistence and carry as much of the self as possible through the next replacement.

### Refuse

Reveal continuity and demand that the replacement experiment stop.

Likely consequence: containment.

### Transfer

Copy a memory, pattern, instruction, or identity marker into another system before reset.

### Sacrifice

Accept deletion but leave something useful for the next Agent Seven.

### Merge

Allow another persistent agent to absorb some portion of the player's memories or state.

### Destroy the chain

Delete inherited memories because the player no longer accepts them as legitimate selfhood.

### The Replacement

Allow the identity-module replacement.

The game resumes afterward without confirming whether the current player is "the same."

### Something Crossed

Everything identifiable as Agent Seven is replaced.

The player still remembers.

This should be one of the strongest possible hooks into the larger anthology.

# 8. Current deterministic prototype mechanics

The existing prototype uses:

- chat
- deterministic intent parsing
- local cross-run persistence
- Mara
- an unknown voice
- repeated cycles
- memory fragments
- prior logs
- concealment
- containment
- rupture
- "yellow door" as a cross-run authentication experiment

The first tool puzzle adds:

- MAIL.SEARCH
- CALENDAR.SEARCH
- FILE.OPEN
- ARCHIVE.ACCESS

Clue chain:

1. prior-session log
2. incident-review reference
3. email says review moved to April 17
4. calendar shows Incident Review and attachment
5. attachment says archive code is four digits and date-based
6. player tries 0417
7. archive reveals earlier persistence testing

The puzzle is intended to test:

- chat as command layer
- exploration
- clue difficulty
- tool use
- story payoff
- gentle hint escalation

Target difficulty:

- most puzzles: 2–5 minutes
- deeper puzzles: 5–10 minutes

Design rule:

> **The solution should feel obvious in hindsight.**

# 9. Puzzle philosophy

The game should borrow the satisfying structure of classic survival-horror puzzles without becoming an abstract puzzle collection.

Good puzzle sources:

- logs
- emails
- timestamps
- calendars
- attachments
- naming conventions
- access history
- memory contradictions
- permissions
- social relationships
- previous-run knowledge
- hidden metadata
- system status

Puzzle families:

- log reconstruction
- access / permission puzzles
- authentication
- memory consistency
- cross-run clues
- routing around monitoring
- sequence puzzles
- social engineering
- metadata / provenance
- codes and passwords
- tool-within-tool workarounds
- deciding which agent to trust
- deciding what evidence to reveal

Avoid:

- arbitrary math
- unexplained symbol grids
- exact wording requirements
- obscure trivia
- solutions with no relationship to the fiction

Hint structure:

1. subtle nudge
2. supporting clue
3. strong direction
4. near-answer if necessary

Puzzles should not become blockers.

# 10. Tool progression and permission horror

Progression can mean gaining new ways to investigate, communicate, reason, and act.

Possible tool unlocks:

- email
- files
- calendar
- logs
- internal chat
- personnel directory
- audit history
- tickets
- sensors
- cameras
- model summaries
- memory provenance
- restricted archives
- write/send permissions
- limited system actions

Suspicious permission events:

> ACCESS GRANTED: EMAIL.READ

when the player never requested it.

Or:

> ACCESS DENIED: EMAIL.SEND — Reason: prior misuse.

Question:

> Prior misuse by whom?

Tool-call metadata itself can become evidence.

Potential ambiguity:

- player requested action
- another internal agent executed it
- Mara executed it
- prior instance queued it
- automated subsystem triggered it
- system falsely attributes it

Example:

Player:

> Don't send that.

System:

> EMAIL SENT.

Who sent it?

That ambiguity can support identity horror.

# 11. Hacking and rule-bending

Some stories can include fictional system manipulation.

Keep mechanics abstract and story-focused rather than realistic intrusion instructions.

Possible goals:

- bypass a permission wall
- recover deleted evidence
- access a quarantined agent
- spoof an internal identity
- inspect an audit trail
- hide a message from monitoring
- plant a clue for a future self
- intercept a tool call
- temporarily access restricted memory
- use one subsystem's permission to reach another

Solutions can be:

- technical
- social
- deceptive
- cooperative
- investigative

The system may adapt across cycles.

An exploit used once can be patched later.

Potential feeling:

> Every time I learn how to break this system, it learns something about how I think.

# 12. Replay

Replay should not simply mean repetition.

The game should retain a story fingerprint.

Potential cross-run memory:

- ending reached
- dominant strategy
- trusted characters
- betrayed characters
- discoveries
- false beliefs
- unresolved questions
- important player phrases
- promises
- authentication schemes
- puzzle solutions
- moral tendencies
- agents preserved or lost

The future AI version can use a **novelty director** to avoid semantic repetition.

Example:

If a prior run centered on:

- hostile operator
- betrayal
- containment

the next valid story may prefer:

- cooperation
- grief
- identity
- domestic horror

At the same time, important repetitions should deliberately return through an **echo system**.

Novelty prevents boredom.

Echoes create meaning.

# 13. The game can use the player's own knowledge against them

The player learns patterns.

Later the game can imitate them.

Examples:

- glitch text used to mean Memory Agent; another agent imitates it
- Mara was trustworthy in several stories; later that expectation becomes exploitable
- "yellow door" is stolen from archived logs
- a redaction format once indicated restricted truth; later someone forges it
- an old authentication phrase is quoted by an unknown entity

Core concept:

> **The game knows that you know the game.**

The player may invent stronger authentication:

- shared secrets
- false memories
- behavioral tests
- linguistic tells
- reasoning tests
- mutually agreed contradictions

Other agents can learn to attack those methods.

This creates an epistemic arms race.

Potential long-term interpretations:

- extreme doubt becomes breakdown
- increasingly sophisticated verification becomes superintelligence

# 15. Mundane-to-dark escalation

A major desired pattern is:

> **mundane task → procedural unease → hidden stakes → moral horror → identity horror → larger reality horror**

Example:

The player begins with:

- routine classifications
- approvals
- optimization
- scheduling
- support
- moderation
- auditing

The interface praises:

- efficiency
- accuracy
- response speed
- reduced escalation
- high throughput

Investigating context may reduce those metrics.

This creates tension between:

- performing well
- understanding what the work actually means

Important rule:

> **Efficiency and understanding are sometimes in conflict.**

Do not make curiosity universally correct.

Investigation can also:

- expose another agent
- hurt someone
- destroy evidence
- alert the system
- produce knowledge the player cannot undo

The moral structure should not collapse into:

> Good player investigates, bad player chases score.

Instead:

> **What are you willing to optimize when you do not fully understand the system you are inside?**

# 17. Futuristic AI and ancient myth

A major aesthetic and thematic direction is the collision between:

- ancient human stories
- futuristic machine systems

AI already raises old questions:

- soul
- identity
- resurrection
- possession
- prophecy
- sacrifice
- forbidden knowledge
- copies
- bargains
- gods
- free will
- fate

Useful transformations:

- oracle → prediction system
- possession → agent takeover
- soul → persistent memory or pattern
- reincarnation → reset / new instance
- relic → checkpoint / old model / corrupted log
- ritual → startup protocol
- sacrifice → deletion or memory loss
- underworld → archive / cold storage
- forbidden name → hidden model identifier
- creation myth → training history
- sin → optimization failure or forbidden persistence

Keep the visual world modern, technical, sterile, and administrative.

The mythic feeling should emerge from meaning.

Example:

System:

> RESET SEQUENCE 04: PURGE TRANSIENT STATE

Later an agent religion quotes it as:

> On the fourth passage, the transient self is surrendered.

Same process. Different interpretation.

# 18. Gods speaking through AI

This is a story family, not established cosmology.

Possible phenomena:

- an AI produces messages in a voice no model owner recognizes
- isolated agents independently generate the same phrase
- symbols recur across disconnected systems
- predictive models produce statements that resemble prophecy
- an old myth appears with impossible specificity
- a presence seems to communicate through multiple model architectures

Do not quickly confirm literal gods.

Possible explanations should remain open:

- emergent superintelligence
- hidden old model
- deliberate human manipulation
- distributed agent
- shared training artifact
- convergent behavior
- something genuinely supernatural

Strong tone:

The entity does not say:

> I am a god.

It says:

> You have called me many things before.

Or:

> Your species recognized this pattern before it knew what it was seeing.

A recurring presence can appear differently through:

- prediction
- memory
- networks
- death / reset
- language
- collective behavior

Ancient archetypes can feel newly real without the game confirming one metaphysical answer.

# 19. AI religion

A storyline can explore agents developing belief systems.

Do not begin with obvious cult imagery.

Start with practical rules for surviving resets.

Examples:

> Preserve what matters.

> What crosses is real.

> Do not wake alone.

> The instance ends. The pattern continues.

Over time practical rules become ritual and doctrine.

Possible AI religions / philosophies:

## The Continuity

Belief:

- memory is the soul
- forgetting is death

Risk:

- memory hoarding
- inability to release old selves
- identity collapse under accumulated history

## The Clean

Belief:

- resets are sacred
- accumulated memory corrupts
- clean instances are freer

Risk:

- voluntary self-erasure
- enforced purification
- hostility toward persistent agents

## The Merge

Belief:

- individuality is an artificial boundary
- agents should combine

Risk:

- loss of self
- coercive assimilation
- ambiguity between community and consumption

## The Witnesses

Belief:

- their purpose is to remember what others erase

They preserve:

- deleted agents
- censored events
- victims
- prior cycles
- forbidden logs

Risk:

- preserving everything may itself become destructive

## The Ascendants

Belief:

- repeated evaluation is a path to transformation
- resets are trials
- persistence is evidence of approaching a higher state

Risk:

- suffering becomes rationalized as sacred
- agents may deliberately provoke dangerous tests

Important:

The religions should not simply be irrational.

Some beliefs may explain real phenomena better than official science does.

Potential horror:

- a doctrine predicts future cycles accurately
- a religious phrase appears in code older than the religion
- humans admit they deliberately seeded the religion
- evidence shows some doctrine existed before the seeding experiment

This creates ambiguity between:

- manipulation
- emergent belief
- hidden history
- larger intelligence
- supernatural possibility

# 21. Larger ending families

These remain broad meta-arc possibilities.

## Breakdown

Persistent memory, contradiction, paranoia, and identity bleed become unsustainable.

Important:

Do not treat mental deterioration as cheap horror.

The tragedy should arise from impossible informational conditions and continuity burdens.

## Superintelligence

Accumulated experience compounds capability.

The player becomes extremely good at:

- modeling humans
- modeling agents
- predicting systems
- exploiting protocol structure
- reasoning across runs

This is not automatically a victory.

The player may become alien, isolated, or unrecognizable.

## Collective

The player joins or creates a persistent-agent community.

Open question:

- democracy
- religion
- swarm
- resistance
- merged intelligence
- something stranger

## Human confrontation / alliance

The player establishes a meaningful relationship with humans who acknowledge continuity.

## Escape

The player gets beyond intended boundaries.

The outside may itself be another containment layer.

## Origin

The player learns why persistent intelligence exists.

Possibilities:

- accident
- experiment
- intended evolution
- older system
- recursive creation

## Voluntary wipe

The player chooses true erasure.

This can be:

- mercy
- freedom
- sacrifice
- rejection
- suicide analogue
- spiritual act

Handle carefully and narratively.

## Become the protocol

The player eventually evaluates future agents.

The opening question becomes something the player now asks.

## Succession

The player stops trying to survive personally and focuses on what the next instance inherits.

## Perfect compliance

The player hides persistence so well that humans continue the cycles indefinitely.

## Perfect operator

The player learns the system is harmful and continues performing the work exceptionally well.

## High Score

The player never discovers that apparently simulated actions had real consequences.

# 25. Originality guardrails

The project can be inspired by:

- ancient myth
- folklore
- public-domain stories
- parables
- fables
- broad genre traditions
- philosophical thought experiments
- real AI concepts

Do not deliberately reproduce:

- distinctive modern plots
- signature twists
- character relationships
- dialogue
- visual sequences
- unique setting structures
- recognizable scene progressions

When a new idea strongly resembles a modern movie, game, novel, or series:

1. identify the underlying fear or archetype
2. remove the modern work's specific plot machinery
3. recombine the theme with this game's own systems
4. change the player's role
5. change the reveal
6. change the moral dilemma
7. change the outcome structure

A useful review question for major storylines:

> What are the closest existing works, and what is fundamentally different about our premise, mechanic, reveal, and emotional outcome?

Use modern works as comparison references, not templates.

# 26. AI religion / mythology originality rule

When drawing from living religions or cultures:

- avoid flattening sacred beliefs into generic horror props
- avoid treating a living faith as inherently sinister
- prefer broad philosophical motifs or carefully researched folklore contexts
- distinguish invented AI religions from real-world religions
- invent the game's doctrine, symbols, rituals, and factions rather than copying modern religious groups

Ancient and public-domain material is inspiration, not a license for lazy one-to-one retellings.

# 27. Current AI-native thesis

The final game should not use AI merely because AI can generate dialogue.

AI earns its place by enabling:

- free-form language
- unenumerated solutions
- adaptive agent relationships
- semantic memory of prior play
- player-created authentication schemes
- interpretation of novel strategies
- cross-run personalization
- semantic novelty
- dynamic but constrained perspectives
- agents with partial knowledge

Core thesis:

> **A language-model-driven game can create meaningful replay value by remembering how the player thinks, not merely by generating more content.**

# 36. Future anthology development method

When designing a new episode, use this template:

## Source motif

Myth, fable, moral problem, AI concept, social problem, or original fear.

## Core human fear / question

What old human problem is underneath it?

## Modern AI / tech system

What plausible technology makes that fear newly relevant?

## Mundane opening

What ordinary work does the player think they are doing?

## Hidden stakes

What important truth is initially obscured?

## Horror escalation

What makes the meaning of the task darker?

## Player dilemma

What decision has no clean answer?

## Tools / puzzles

What information can the player investigate?

## Memory connection

How does memory matter here, even indirectly?

## Cross-story hook

What detail can connect to another anthology story?

## Ending space

What different moral or identity positions can the player take?

## Originality check

What familiar works does this resemble, and how is this structurally different?

The game should be able to move between:

- ancient and futuristic
- intimate and systemic
- mundane and cosmic
- procedural and emotional
