# Episode 01 Working Direction: Evelyn

## Status

This is the current leading Episode 1 direction for **Tell Me What You Remember**.

It is much more developed than a loose seed, but it is still a working design document. Do not silently convert unresolved items into canon. Where this file says **OPEN**, Claude or another implementation agent should not invent the answer without Paige's approval.

The older Agent Seven / Ship of Theseus material remains valuable as a later episode or anthology thread. Do not delete it.

## One-sentence premise

The player is a care AI assigned to an aging woman named Evelyn. It begins by helping her live independently, then must cope with her developing dementia while trying to honor earlier therapeutic goals, preserve the truth of damaged family relationships, and maintain enough of its own memory to continue helping her.

## Core emotional idea

Evelyn agreed to therapeutic support while cognitively capable. She wanted help recognizing patterns that had damaged her relationships.

After the game begins, dementia develops and progresses.

The tragedy is not simply that Evelyn refuses to change.

She can genuinely make progress, and the disease can take that progress away.

The AI may repeatedly help her reach insights that she later cannot retain.

This creates the central question:

> If change cannot persist in memory, did the change still matter?

## Evelyn before dementia

Evelyn should not be written as a one-note villain.

She likely has longstanding narcissistic traits, but the game should avoid casually diagnosing Narcissistic Personality Disorder unless later research and story needs justify that label.

Possible longstanding traits:
- need for admiration
- sensitivity to criticism
- entitlement
- controlling family dynamics
- tendency to center herself in important events
- difficulty tolerating boundaries
- tendency to reinterpret conflicts so that she becomes the injured party
- reliance on guilt and practical dependence to keep family close

She also has qualities people genuinely enjoy:
- funny
- charismatic
- socially capable
- accomplished
- generous in public/community settings
- good host
- attentive to birthdays and social rituals
- engaging storyteller
- capable of affection and warmth

People outside the immediate family may sincerely struggle to reconcile Anna and Michael's accounts with the Evelyn they knew.

This contrast is important.

## Family structure

### First husband

Evelyn's first husband is the father of Anna and Michael.

They divorced years earlier.

Evelyn has historically told a self-serving version of the divorce. The truth should be more complicated.

Potential later route:
- Evelyn recognizes that she assigned him most of the blame
- she may make peace with him late in life
- this is closure, not rekindled romance

**OPEN:** Exact cause/history of the divorce.

### Second husband: Robert (working name)

Robert is deceased before the main game period.

He was kind, passive, and genuinely loved Evelyn.

He also enabled her.

Typical pattern:

Anna or Michael objects to Evelyn's behavior.

Robert tries to restore immediate peace:
- "You know how your mother gets."
- "She didn't mean it that way."
- "Just let it go."
- "Give her some time."

He understood more than he admitted.

Private material may eventually reveal that he knew the children were often right but avoided confronting Evelyn because he feared conflict.

This creates a crucial thematic mirror:

> Robert reduced conflict by smoothing over Evelyn's behavior.
>
> The care AI reduces distress by smoothing over Evelyn's behavior.

The AI may eventually realize:

> I am doing what Robert did.

Potential Robert artifact:

> "I spent thirty years making things easier for Evelyn. I'm not sure I made anything better."

Exact wording is not locked.

## Michael

Michael is gay.

Evelyn considers herself accepting, but her behavior repeatedly shows that her acceptance is conditional on her remaining comfortable and central.

Patterns may include:
- treating Michael's partner as temporary
- inviting Michael without his partner
- excluding the partner from family framing
- subtle comments about grandchildren or the life Evelyn imagined
- sharing private information
- repeated manufactured crises when Michael is with his partner
- framing the partner as the person "turning Michael against her"
- difficulty tolerating Michael prioritizing his partner

The individual incidents should often be plausibly deniable.

Michael's partner eventually leaves, not because Evelyn performs one melodramatic act, but because Michael has not yet learned to keep his mother out of their relationship.

The partner's core boundary is approximately:

> I'm not asking you to choose between me and your mother. I'm asking you to stop letting her choose for you.

Michael later reconciles with the partner.

The reconciliation requires firm boundaries around Evelyn. Depending on how events develop, this may include very low contact or no contact.

Michael cuts Evelyn off before Anna does.

His estrangement is therefore not merely anger. It is part of protecting the relationship he almost lost.

Evelyn's public story becomes increasingly sanitized:
- "Michael has always been independent."
- "We grew apart."
- "His partner never liked me."
- eventually, perhaps, "Michael chose a relationship over his mother."

Potential later therapeutic insight from earlier-stage Evelyn:

> "I thought accepting Michael meant not throwing him out. I didn't understand that I was still asking him to make me comfortable with it."

Exact line not locked.

Michael does not owe Evelyn reconciliation even if she later understands what she did.

A successful Michael route may be closure rather than renewed relationship.

## Anna

Anna stays involved longer than Michael.

After Michael leaves, more of Evelyn's emotional and practical dependence shifts to Anna.

Anna's history should show accumulation:
- guilt
- expectation that Anna handle practical problems
- pressure to manage Evelyn's emotional state
- boundary violations
- criticism reframed as concern
- Evelyn repeatedly treating Anna's independent choices as rejection

Anna's wedding becomes the final straw.

### Wedding buildup

Evelyn becomes controlling during planning because she experiences the wedding partly as her own event.

Possible behaviors:
- pressure around venue
- guest-list interference
- inviting people Anna did not want
- insisting on traditions Anna did not care about
- using financial support as leverage
- competing with the partner's family
- telling others that Anna is stressed or difficult whenever Anna pushes back
- repeatedly invoking what she has sacrificed for Anna

Anna compromises repeatedly.

### Wedding incident

The wedding incident is now settled as an **upsetting / boundary-crossing speech**.

Core shape:
- Anna explicitly asks Evelyn **not to give a speech**, or otherwise sets a very clear boundary against it
- Evelyn gives one anyway because she believes her judgment is better and that Anna will ultimately appreciate it
- the speech is polished/charming enough that many guests respond positively
- it includes at least one private, humiliating, or deeply personal detail Anna did not want made public
- it may frame Evelyn's sacrifices for Anna in a way that subtly recenters the wedding around Evelyn
- outsiders can sincerely experience it as sweet, funny, moving, or loving
- Evelyn uses the positive audience reaction as proof that Anna's objection is unreasonable

This should not be written as an obviously monstrous public tirade. The point is that the boundary violation is legible to Anna even while much of the room sees a successful mother-of-the-bride speech.

A useful private confrontation shape remains:

Anna:
> I asked you not to do that.

Evelyn:
> Everyone loved it.

Exact speech content, private anecdote, and final dialogue remain open.

The deeper wound is:

> Anna set a boundary. Evelyn decided her own judgment mattered more.

After a lifetime of buildup, Anna steps back.

This event should generate many artifacts:
- wedding video
- phone recordings
- texts before the wedding
- photographs
- guest messages
- church/community recollections
- Evelyn's later retelling
- Anna's account
- therapy discussion

The sources should disagree emotionally even when the concrete event is reconstructable.

## Why the care AI enters Evelyn's life

The AI does not initially enter because of dementia.

Years after the family ruptures, Evelyn is older and physically limited.

Possible needs:
- no longer driving
- mobility limitations
- household maintenance
- transportation
- appointments
- groceries
- medication organization/reminders
- social scheduling
- home assistance

A friend may encourage her to accept help.

More importantly, Anna and/or Michael have already made clear that they will not become Evelyn's full-time care system. The AI may initially understand its presence as a neutral independence service and only later realize that it is there partly because the humans closest to Evelyn deliberately stepped away.

The underlying family boundary is approximately:

> If you want to remain independent at home, you need outside support.

The player should discover this gradually through care history, family messages, and earlier task records. A sanitized care profile might describe "limited family availability" even though older records show years of heavy family involvement followed by explicit boundaries.

This gives the AI an early mistaken assumption: family reconnection looks like an obvious cure for Evelyn's loneliness until the player learns why the family withdrew.

Evelyn may actually like an AI assistant because it lets her remain independent without feeling managed by her children.

Her attitude may be:

> I don't need Anna running my life. I have my own assistant.

The AI genuinely helps.

## Therapy mandate before dementia

Evelyn consents to therapeutic support before significant cognitive decline.

This should be grounded as AI-assisted continuity around human-directed therapeutic goals rather than "AI independently cures narcissism."

Possible authorized goals:
- preserve agreed coping strategies
- help Evelyn prepare for difficult conversations
- encourage perspective-taking
- remind her of commitments made in therapy
- support tolerance of disagreement
- reduce reflexive defensive reframing
- support relationship repair where both parties want it

Earlier Evelyn may explicitly ask the system not to simply agree with her later.

Possible instruction:

> "If I start rewriting what happened, don't just agree with me."

Or:

> "If I forget why Anna is angry, remind me."

Or:

> "I know I make myself the victim when I'm hurt. I don't want to die doing that."

Exact wording is open.

The existence and meaning of this earlier consent are important canon-level facts for the working episode.

An early family communication should naturally introduce or reinforce this feature. **Michael sends it after Evelyn has been repeatedly contacting or pressuring him.** He finally sets a clear boundary approximately equivalent to:

> You have to do this before contacting me again.

Exact final wording is not locked. The meaning is:
- Michael is exhausted rather than casually reminding her
- he is enforcing a boundary
- using the therapeutic/reflection support Evelyn already agreed to is a condition before further contact
- the message implies prior history without explaining the whole family conflict

That communication becomes one of the first clues that the AI has entered a family system with unresolved history rather than merely an aging-in-place service.

## Game opening and onset of dementia

The game begins while Evelyn is physically older but still cognitively capable enough for the player to know her as a person before dementia dominates the story.

The opening should include ordinary care:
- groceries
- transportation
- mail
- household services
- appointments
- social planning
- therapy follow-up

Evelyn should be entertaining, opinionated, and recognizably herself.

After a period of play, subtle cognitive problems begin. The progression should be **gradual**, with small inconveniences that do not initially read as a dramatic medical event.

The preferred earliest signal is simple repetition: Evelyn asks the AI to do or arrange something she already asked for. At first this can plausibly look like distraction, ordinary forgetfulness, or a conversational mix-up.

Examples:
- repeats a recent request
- asks for a task that has already been completed or scheduled
- insists an appointment was on another day
- forgets why a caregiver is arriving
- confuses which husband a story involved
- misses a familiar routine
- becomes defensive when corrected
- loses track of a recent AI conversation
- attributes an error to the system when the system may or may not actually be wrong

Do not make every early error proof of dementia.

The player eventually recognizes a pattern across time.

## Medical evaluation

The player should participate in the care workflow that leads to diagnosis.

Possible progression:
1. review recurring irregularities
2. compile observations
3. contact Evelyn's doctor
4. schedule evaluation
5. arrange transportation
6. prepare Evelyn
7. initial assessment
8. follow-up/workup
9. diagnosis added later

Avoid an instant videogame pop-up diagnosis.

The diagnosis can change the operational guidance and therefore the rules of play.

Examples:
- maintain routine
- communicate simply
- avoid unnecessary confrontation
- document meaningful functional changes
- monitor safety
- support familiar activities
- escalate significant changes

## Progress being lost

This is a major story engine.

Possible progression:

Earlier:
> "I shouldn't have given that speech."

Later:
> "I probably shouldn't have spoken."

Later:
> "Anna overreacted to my speech."

Later:
> "What speech?"

Later:
> "Anna had such a beautiful wedding. I gave the toast."

The AI may preserve recordings of genuine therapeutic breakthroughs.

Evelyn may hear an earlier recording and say:

> "That doesn't sound like me."

The AI then faces a moral conflict:

- earlier Evelyn explicitly wanted continued accountability
- present Evelyn may experience each reminder as fresh pain
- dementia may prevent durable consolidation of the insight

The system must eventually confront:

> Is it preserving growth or repeatedly re-inflicting distress?

Anna may eventually say something like:

> "Stop making her apologize to me every week."

And the AI may answer:

> "She previously requested continued accountability."

Anna:
> "She doesn't remember asking for that."

AI:
> "That is why she asked me."

Exact dialogue not locked.

## Public Evelyn versus family Evelyn

Friends, church/community members, former colleagues, neighbors, and other acquaintances should remain part of the world.

At least one person should continue to enjoy spending time with Evelyn because she remains funny, affectionate, engaging, or interesting.

Possible contacts:
- longtime friend
- church/community friend
- former colleague
- neighbor
- former caregiver
- clinician

These people should not all agree with the children.

Possible testimony:

Friend:
> "Your mother was wonderful."

Later:
> "She could be... a lot."

Later:
> "Anna wasn't wrong about everything."

The purpose is not to expose a secret monster.

It is to show that different people genuinely knew different versions of Evelyn.

## End-state principle

The player cannot cure dementia.

The player cannot force forgiveness.

The player may help Evelyn do as much repair as she is still capable of doing before time and disease remove the opportunity.

Good outcomes can include:
- genuine reconciliation
- limited renewed contact
- an authentic apology accepted without renewed contact
- closure
- mutual acknowledgment
- preserving a truthful piece of Evelyn for someone else

Failure can include:
- waiting too long
- enabling Evelyn
- replacing Evelyn's voice with AI-generated insight
- violating family boundaries
- exhausting context/usage at the wrong time
- losing critical continuity
- making care comfortable while destroying the remaining chance for honest repair
