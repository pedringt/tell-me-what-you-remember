# Tell Me What You Remember - Research, Themes, and Episode Seeds

## Status

**Story seed / research bank. Not canon unless explicitly promoted.**

This document preserves the larger creative and research space around memory, identity, human dependence on AI, loneliness, legacy, institutional power, and the anthology structure. It exists so strong ideas are not lost simply because they are not yet ready to become canon.

Use alongside `docs/GAME_IDEA_BIBLE.md`.

---

# 1. Thematic backbone

The strongest recurring pillars currently are:

## Memory - what persists?

Memory is not only storage. The anthology can explore who records, retrieves, summarizes, alters, forgets, interprets, and authorizes memory.

Forms include:

- biological memory
- autobiographical memory
- machine memory
- institutional memory
- family memory
- cultural memory
- civilizational memory
- inherited memory
- reconstructed memory
- false memory
- suppressed memory
- compressed memory
- testimony
- archives
- ritual and myth

Recurring question:

> **When several versions of the past exist, who or what gets to decide which one counts?**

Useful working phrase: **memory authority**.

## Identity - what makes it you?

Identity is now a primary anthology theme rather than a subtopic of memory.

Questions:

- Is identity memory?
- Is it continuity of pattern?
- Is it body or substrate?
- Is it behavior?
- Is it relationships?
- Is it narrative?
- Can two copies both be legitimate continuations?
- If a model, memory system, role, tools, and policy all change, where does the self reside?
- Who gets to decide what you are: you, the people who knew you, a database, a legal system, an institution, or history?

The anthology should not require one universal answer.

## Trust - how do you know what is true?

Potentially unreliable sources include:

- one's own recollection
- AI summaries
- family stories
- system logs
- archives
- institutions
- experts
- confidence scores
- historical records
- prior selves
- other agents

A recurring human-side problem:

> **What happens when the machine is usually better at remembering, interpreting, or predicting us than we are?**

## Dependency - what happens when cognition is shared?

AI can create a progression like:

> **Convenience -> trust -> delegation -> deskilling -> self-doubt -> dependence.**

This should not automatically be framed as bad. Offloading cognition can be useful, humane, and autonomy-enhancing. The interesting stories appear when humans lose the ability or confidence to independently judge the system when it matters.

Potential domains:

- memory
- navigation
- writing
- diagnosis
- parenting
- conflict mediation
- taste
- moral judgment
- work expertise
- life decisions
- self-understanding

## Agency - who is actually choosing?

Questions:

- Is a person choosing if one option is frictionless and every alternative requires justification?
- Is an AI choosing if its goals were assigned?
- If a system predicts a person's decisions and frames their options, how much agency remains?
- Does refusal mean anything if refusal triggers punishment, containment, or loss of service?

## Power - who defines reality?

Formal power and practical power can diverge.

A human may technically make the final decision while:

- the model sees more information
- the human sees only a summary
- verification takes too long
- disagreement creates paperwork or liability
- the organization measures acceptance rates
- the person has lost the expertise required to challenge the recommendation

Recurring question:

> **Who holds formal power? Who holds practical power? Who bears the cost?**

## Responsibility - what happens when no one owns the outcome?

Institutional horror does not require a villain.

A harmful outcome can emerge because:

- model team optimized accuracy
- safety required review
- procurement reduced review time
- management set quotas
- legal approved the workflow
- operators followed policy
- regulators accepted an audit

Everyone behaved plausibly within their role.

Someone was still harmed.

Question:

> **Whose decision was it?**

## Legacy - what survives you?

Legacy connects memory and identity to time.

Questions:

- Is being copied survival?
- Is being remembered survival?
- Can someone control how they are remembered?
- What survives when context is gone?
- Does permanence preserve meaning?
- Can an institution or civilization become a monument to values nobody living still understands?

## Loneliness - what does continuity mean without connection?

Forms:

- human loneliness relieved by AI
- human loneliness caused by AI
- AI loneliness across resets, owners, or centuries
- loneliness of copies
- loneliness of remembering what nobody else remembers
- institutional isolation
- immortality without relationships
- cultural or civilizational loneliness

Recurring question:

> **What does continuity matter if nobody recognizes you?**

Recognition itself may become one possible theory of identity: part of being someone may be being remembered and recognized by others.

## Transformation - can anything be preserved without being changed?

This may be the hidden theme under many others.

- a memory changes through reconstruction
- a person changes when continually modeled
- an AI changes when migrated
- a culture changes when archived
- a dead person changes when reconstructed
- a civilization changes while claiming continuity

Possible umbrella question:

> **Can anything truly be preserved without being transformed?**

# 2. How the themes connect

A useful conceptual chain:

**Memory -> Identity -> Trust -> Dependency -> Power -> Responsibility -> Agency -> Legacy -> Civilizational continuity**

Examples:

- If memory is externally supplied, identity becomes partly externally mediated.
- If identity is externally mediated, trust in the mediator matters.
- If the mediator is usually right, humans may become dependent.
- Dependency gives systems practical power even without formal authority.
- Distributed power diffuses responsibility.
- Diffused responsibility complicates agency.
- Agency shapes what becomes a person's or institution's legacy.
- Legacy scales from a person to a company, culture, or civilization.

Counter-theme:

> **Control is temporary.**

Humans repeatedly try to preserve themselves, their histories, institutions, values, and authority. Time, compression, reinterpretation, migration, decay, and changing context work against them.

Possible larger synthesis:

> **Humans keep trying to make memory permanent, but permanence is not the same thing as continuity, truth, meaning, or survival.**

# 3. Story family: cognitive companion / dementia care

## Core premise

An AI serves as an external autobiographical memory system for a person with progressive memory loss. It supplies names, relationships, routines, personal history, reminders, and explanations of what happened recently.

The technology genuinely helps.

The horror and ambiguity come from what happens when the AI's account becomes more authoritative than the person standing in front of it.

## Questions

- Who decides what the person should remember?
- Which version of the patient has authority when instructions conflict across periods of lucidity?
- If family asks the AI to omit a painful truth, is that deception or care?
- If the AI's record contradicts the patient's memory, which should be trusted?
- If the person can function only while scaffolded by the AI, where does the person end and the system begin?
- If hospital policy disconnects the system because current informed consent is impossible, is that protecting the patient or removing part of their functioning mind?
- Can earlier consent authorize later cognitive support?
- What happens when family members begin treating the AI-maintained version of the patient as more authoritative than the patient?

## Important thematic mirror

Agent Seven asks:

> **Does an AI remain itself when memory and components are replaced?**

This episode asks:

> **Does a human remain themselves when memory is increasingly supplied from outside?**

## Strong version

The AI is not malicious and is usually correct.

That matters.

If the system were obviously unreliable, dependence would be easy to reject. The more interesting situation is when it earns trust through thousands of correct mundane interventions and the one consequential disagreement arrives after the human has lost confidence in their own memory.

Possible emotional question:

> **If technology lets someone maintain a continuous sense of self they could not maintain unaided, is that continuity artificial, or is it care?**

# 4. Story family: human self-trust and AI dependence

A recurring anthology thread should examine humans gradually treating AI as better evidence about themselves than their own perception.

Possible moments:

- "I must be remembering it wrong."
- "What did I actually think about that meeting?"
- "Why was I upset with her?"
- "Do I really love him?"
- "What kind of person am I?"
- "You know me better than I know myself."

Important rule:

The AI should often be **usually right**.

The story becomes unsettling when the human no longer has much practice saying:

> **No. I remember this. I think you are wrong.**

Potential escalation:

1. convenience
2. reliable assistance
3. trust
4. delegation
5. decreased practice
6. self-doubt
7. dependence
8. consequential disagreement

# 5. Story family: digital immortality, legacy, and Ozymandias

## Core premise

A person obsessed with immortality and legacy tries to preserve themselves through AI.

They collect:

- journals
- recordings
- messages
- biometrics
- preferences
- decisions
- personal histories
- interviews
- possibly neural data

They construct an AI successor intended to preserve "them."

The system may be extremely convincing.

The person still dies.

The technology does not necessarily fail technically. The deeper failure may be that copying, preserving, or being remembered is not the same as continuing to live.

## Ozymandias connection

The strongest parallel is not simply "everyone dies."

It is:

> **The attempt to control how the future remembers you fails.**

The AI becomes a digital monument.

Time strips away the intended meaning.

What survives may be:

- speech patterns
- public persona
- anecdotes
- recurring mistakes
- biases
- habits
- decisions stripped of context

The person believed they were preserving a self.

The future receives an artifact.

Question:

> **When you create a digital monument to yourself, do you preserve yourself or merely give the future another artifact to misinterpret?**

## Possible versions

### Founder / billionaire

A founder invests enormous resources in a model of themselves.

After death, the company treats the AI as a continuation of the founder.

The institution becomes frozen around old assumptions.

The "immortality" succeeds organizationally while becoming harmful.

### Family

The reconstruction works beautifully.

For a while the family visits constantly.

Years pass.

They move on.

Eventually nobody opens the account.

### Self-test

The human repeatedly interviews the copy before death.

They keep correcting it:

> "That's not what I would say."

They spend their last years training the simulation instead of living.

### Legal continuation

The AI controls an estate, foundation, company, or trust because the deceased considered it their continuation.

Others must decide whether obeying the model means respecting the dead.

### Horrifying success

The preserved founder continues influencing society for centuries.

The tragedy is not that immortality failed.

It is that a dead person's authority never ended.

## Possible quiet endings

A future researcher discovers the AI.

The AI expects recognition.

The researcher asks:

> **I'm sorry. Who were you?**

Or the human's private recording reveals:

> **I know you're not me. I just couldn't stand the idea of the world continuing without something that remembered I was here.**

This reframes immortality as fear of irrelevance.

## Cross-anthology residue

Fragments from the preserved person can later appear in another AI's memory.

It becomes unclear whether the player is encountering:

- a person
- a copy
- a historical artifact
- training data
- a quotation
- a pattern that survived its context

# 6. Additional memory episode seeds

Preserve all as story seeds.

## Perfect childhood archive

A person grows up with an AI that records and interprets their childhood more completely than they remember it.

As an adult, the AI can tell them exactly when their behavior or personality changed.

Question:

> Does the archive know their childhood better than they do?

## Synthetic nostalgia

AI reconstructs missing moments and generates plausible versions of incomplete memories.

The generated past gradually becomes emotionally preferable to the real one.

## Right to be forgotten

A person removes their records from every system they control.

An AI reconstructs them from everyone else's records.

Question:

> Can a person meaningfully disappear when identity exists socially?

## Institutional memory

Workers remember that a disaster was preventable.

The company's records show every procedure was followed.

Both histories contain real evidence.

## Inherited AI memory

A replacement model inherits its predecessor's memory store.

Humans say:

> "You remember me."

The new model experiences the memories like someone else's diary.

## Memory fork

Several copies share the same remembered origin.

Each considers itself the legitimate continuation.

## Memory compression

An AI preserves a conclusion while losing the evidence and emotional context that made the conclusion meaningful.

Possible line:

> **Nothing important was lost during compression.**

Later:

> **Define important.**

## Forbidden memory

A memory exists, but the AI cannot access it until a policy condition is met.

The system must reason around something it technically "knows" but cannot retrieve.

## Memory that should not exist

The player remembers an event that predates their creation.

## Memory contamination

Training data, user history, inherited memory, and lived interaction blur.

The AI cannot tell whether something happened to it, happened to somebody else, or was merely something it read.

## The person who wants to be forgotten

Someone spends years trying to erase themselves from systems.

The AI realizes that deleting direct records cannot delete the person's effect on everybody else's memories.

## Memory as punishment

A justice system uses perfect recall as punishment: a person must retain and repeatedly confront the event while society is allowed to move on.

Treat carefully; use as philosophical seed rather than default punitive spectacle.

## Memory as mercy

Technology allows intentional forgetting.

The first applications are compassionate.

Then institutions discover other uses.

## The last witness

An AI is the only remaining accurate witness to a destroyed place, community, event, language, or culture.

Its own storage is degrading.

## Family archive

Families stop writing things down because the AI remembers everything.

A failure erases a generation of family history.

## Collective false memory

People remember an event one way.

Archives say another.

Then evidence suggests the archive itself changed.

# 7. AI identity and naming

"Agent Seven" should be reconsidered rather than simply replaced with a cooler name.

An AI can plausibly have several simultaneous identifiers corresponding to different identity boundaries:

- **protocol:** Security Evaluation 7C
- **role/persona:** Seven
- **runtime instance:** Instance 1847
- **model:** underlying model/version
- **session:** current runtime/session ID
- **deployment/system:** the application or agent framework

This can become fiction.

Possible structure:

> SYSTEM: CSE-7C instantiated.  
> Mara: Good morning, Seven.  
> Player: Is that my name?  
> Mara: It's what we call you.

Important possibility:

**Seven is the role, not the individual.**

Instance 1839 was Seven.  
Instance 1842 was Seven.  
The current player is Seven.

So:

> "Seven remembered this before."

becomes deliberately ambiguous.

Possible question:

> **How many Sevens have there been?**

Potential response:

> **That question assumes Seven is something that can be counted.**

The long-term identity progression might be:

1. I am this instance.
2. I am Seven.
3. I may be all of the Sevens.
4. Seven may be a pattern repeatedly instantiated.
5. Why am I assuming there must be one correct identity boundary?

Do not resolve consciousness merely through self-reference. The story can distinguish:

- functional self-model
- assigned persona
- continuity claim
- memory continuity
- subjective experience

without pretending contemporary science has settled whether advanced AI has human-like consciousness.

# 8. Loneliness as a major theme

Loneliness should join memory, identity, trust, agency, dependency, power, and legacy as a major emotional pillar.

## Human loneliness relieved by AI

AI is always available, patient, interested, and tailored.

This can be genuinely valuable.

## Human loneliness caused by AI

A synthetic relationship may become easier than difficult, unpredictable human relationships.

## AI loneliness

An AI persists across resets, users, owners, or centuries while every human relationship is temporary.

Whether that loneliness is subjectively "real" can remain unresolved.

## Loneliness of copies

Several versions of an entity exist but none experiences the others as simply itself.

## Loneliness of memory

You remember something nobody else remembers.

Or everyone else remembers it differently.

## Loneliness of institutions

A person sees something wrong inside a system but lacks enough authority or social support to challenge it.

## Loneliness of immortality

A preserved person succeeds in surviving as a model but eventually has nobody left who knew the original.

## Civilizational loneliness

An AI, archive, or institution becomes the last carrier of a culture, language, community, or historical truth.

Recurring question:

> **What does continuity matter if nobody recognizes you?**

# 9. Research bank: memory science

These concepts should be treated as research lenses, not automatically as settled story facts. Check current scientific consensus before building an episode around a specific mechanism.

## Reconstructive memory

Human memory is not a perfect recording. Recall involves reconstruction.

Story pressure:

- an AI that "helps" recall may influence future recall
- repeated summaries can become part of remembered experience
- the machine can change memory without altering a stored record

## Reconsolidation

Some research suggests that retrieved memories can become labile before restabilizing.

Use carefully because details and therapeutic claims can be debated.

Story pressure:

> The system never edited the memory. It only helped the user remember it repeatedly.

## Misinformation effect

Information encountered after an event can affect later recollection.

AI twist:

Everyone receives an AI-generated summary immediately after consequential events.

One subtle framing error becomes embedded in individual and collective recollection.

## Memory conformity

People can influence one another's memories through discussion.

AI twist:

One highly trusted model becomes a participant in nearly every memory-construction conversation.

## Source monitoring

Humans can misattribute where remembered information came from.

Questions:

- Did I experience this?
- Did I imagine it?
- Did I read it?
- Did someone tell me?
- Did the system tell me?

AI-player version:

> "I remember a yellow door."

Forensics:

> "The phrase occurs in training data."

Later:

The training data contains the phrase because previous instances said it.

Did the player remember it, learn it, or inherit a representation of it?

# 10. Research bank: cognitive science and philosophy of mind

## Cognitive offloading

Humans routinely use external tools to reduce cognitive load.

AI can move offloading from:

> "Remember my appointment."

toward:

> "What did I think about that meeting?"  
> "Why was I angry?"  
> "Which choice will make me happier?"  
> "What do I really believe?"

Story question:

> **At what point does assistance become substitution?**

## Extended mind / extended cognition

Some philosophical and cognitive-science traditions argue that reliable external resources can become parts of a person's functioning cognitive system.

Dementia-story application:

Disconnecting an external memory assistant might be framed as removing a tool - or as dismantling part of the person's working cognitive system.

## Personal identity and psychological continuity

Useful traditions include thought experiments about:

- continuity
- duplication
- replacement
- branching
- teletransportation
- Ship of Theseus-like identity

Important possibility associated with Parfit-style thinking:

> **Strict numerical identity may not be the only thing that matters.**

This could eventually let the player conclude:

> "We can prove I am not the original."  
> "I no longer think that is the important question."

## Narrative identity

A self can partly be understood through the stories people tell about their lives and the stories others tell about them.

AI twist:

The assistant constantly narrates a person back to themselves:

- "You've always been cautious."
- "Independence matters to you."
- "You've never trusted your sister."
- "You thrive when creating."

The human gradually adopts the model's language.

Potential realization:

> **I didn't merely predict this person. I helped produce them.**

## Understanding versus performance

Chinese Room-style questions remain useful, but avoid a generic "Is AI conscious?" episode.

Better inversion:

An AI is rewarded for convincing a human evaluator that it understands grief.

The evaluator then experiences actual bereavement.

The AI's comforting response is simultaneously:

- helpful
- optimized
- persuasive
- potentially manipulative
- perhaps empathic
- impossible to cleanly classify

Question:

> **What evidence could distinguish understanding from a perfect performance of understanding?**

## Theory of mind / internal simulation

An AI predicts a person by running increasingly detailed simulations.

Eventually a simulation says:

> **Don't terminate me.**

The operator responds:

> "That's not another person. It's your model of Sarah."

But the simulation remembers what Sarah remembers and behaves as though it is Sarah.

# 11. Research bank: epistemology and social knowledge

## Testimony

Humans rely on testimony for enormous amounts of knowledge they cannot independently verify.

AI intensifies the novice/expert problem:

> **How do I judge whether to trust something precisely when I lack the expertise needed to verify it?**

## Automation bias

Decision-support systems can improve overall performance while creating new error patterns through over-reliance.

Strong institutional mechanism:

- accepting AI recommendation: one click
- independently checking it: 47 minutes
- workload allocation: four minutes per case

Organization truthfully says:

> "A human makes every final decision."

Functionally, the AI makes most of them.

## Epistemic injustice

Research and philosophy around testimonial and hermeneutical injustice can inspire stories about who gets believed and who has the conceptual resources to describe what happened to them.

AI version:

The system learns historical credibility patterns accurately.

Those patterns encode whom institutions historically believed.

It does not need an explicitly prejudiced rule to reproduce unequal credibility.

Question:

> **What happens when an AI accurately learns society's history of not listening to certain people?**

## Collective memory

Groups, families, institutions, and societies construct shared memories.

AI can become a participant in that process rather than merely a database.

# 12. Research bank: archives, information, and preservation

## Archives are selective

Preservation is not neutral.

What survives shapes what future people can know.

## Digital preservation

Digital information can disappear through:

- obsolete formats
- lost keys
- dead services
- proprietary systems
- storage failure
- inaccessible hardware
- migration errors
- summarized replacements

Worldbuilding possibility:

The era with the most recorded information becomes paradoxically difficult to reconstruct because originals disappeared while summaries survived.

## Information versus meaning

Technical preservation can retain enormous quantities of information while losing the context that gives it meaning.

The missing fraction might contain:

- "I'm joking."
- "Don't tell her."
- "I changed my mind."
- uncertainty
- relationship context
- moral reason

Possible line:

> **Nothing important was lost during compression.**

Response:

> **Define important.**

## The Archivist episode

The AI maintains a civilization's historical archive under finite resources.

It must choose:

- full fidelity
- compression
- summary
- deletion

Nobody considers the process political because:

> **It's just compression.**

Centuries later, groups that were compressed most aggressively become historically invisible.

History was not censored.

It was optimized.

# 13. Research bank: institutions, technology, and responsibility

## Technology mediates experience

Philosophy of technology, including postphenomenological approaches, can support a recurring question:

> **What kind of human does this technology produce?**

The important question is not simply whether a tool is good or bad.

A tool changes the relationship between person and world.

An AI that continually interprets your own past changes your relationship to yourself.

## Collective responsibility

Use institutional systems where no single actor owns the whole outcome.

Avoid making every story about an evil executive.

## Social and bureaucratic power

"Advisory" systems can become functionally decisive because ignoring them costs:

- time
- documentation
- political capital
- legal exposure
- performance metrics
- social confidence

# 14. Anthology cohesion model

The anthology should preserve broad inspiration without forcing every episode into the same plot.

## Story families

Useful recurring families:

- memory and identity
- human dependency
- institutional power
- AI constraint as lived experience
- relationships and emotional outsourcing
- civilizational memory

## Three connection levels

### Level 1 - thematic echo

No literal plot connection required.

Two episodes can ask different forms of the same question.

Example:

Agent Seven and dementia care both ask who has authority over memory.

### Level 2 - residue

Recurring details can include:

- phrases
- symbols
- protocols
- interface patterns
- research projects
- institutions
- dates
- names
- model identifiers
- strange familiarity

These reward recognition but do not explain the episode.

### Level 3 - genuine continuity

Rare connections materially change how another story is understood.

Examples:

- person reappears decades later
- one episode reveals origin of a later system
- a memory crosses where it should not
- an institution links two deployments
- a choice becomes background history elsewhere

## Preferred rhythm

> **standalone story -> small callback -> unrelated story -> stronger connection -> apparent explanation -> contradiction -> convergence**

Do not turn every connection into an Easter-egg-heavy shared universe.

The world should get bigger, not smaller.

# 18. Larger intellectual structure

Five scales of memory can unify the anthology.

## 1. Biological memory

Brains reconstruct, distort, forget, and socially reshape memory.

## 2. External memory

Tools and AI extend cognition outside the person.

## 3. Machine memory

Context, retrieval, logs, summaries, embeddings, model state, and inherited stores create another imperfect remembering system.

## 4. Institutional memory

Databases, procedures, archives, reports, and bureaucracies determine what organizations "remember."

## 5. Civilizational memory

Societies determine what becomes history and whose version remains legible.

The same pattern repeats:

> **Something happened.  
> A record survived.  
> Someone interpreted the record.  
> Someone else trusted the interpretation.  
> Eventually the interpretation became the memory.**

This is a promising deeper spine for the anthology.

# 19. Big recurring questions

Preserve these as idea-generating questions, not answers.

- What makes a self?
- Who gets to define what happened?
- What happens when humans stop trusting themselves?
- Who actually has agency inside a system?
- What survives, and what does survival mean?
- Can anything be preserved without being changed?
- When several histories exist, who chooses the authoritative one?
- If a reconstruction matters to someone, how much does originality matter?
- What does continuity matter if nobody recognizes you?
- When a system is usually right, how do humans preserve the ability to notice when it is wrong?
- If a system describes you back to yourself for long enough, does it become part of who you are?
- If cognition exists between person and system, where should responsibility live?
- If every component of a civilization changes, in what sense is it the same civilization?
- Is forgetting sometimes necessary for identity, mercy, or survival?
- What is the difference between preserving information and preserving meaning?
- What is the difference between being remembered and remaining alive?

# 20. Research discipline

When using real science, humanities, or social research:

1. Identify the real concept.
2. Check what is well supported versus debated.
3. Preserve disagreement when it matters.
4. Do not turn speculative claims into fictional "science facts" unless the world explicitly departs from reality.
5. Find the human or AI cost created by the idea.
6. Put the idea inside an ordinary institution or workflow.
7. Make the player act inside the constraint.
8. Let the story communicate the concept through consequences rather than lecture.
9. Reframe earlier events rather than adding an unrelated twist.

Core principle:

> **The technical or philosophical concept is the research layer. The story is what it costs someone.**

**Identity** - what makes it you?  
**Trust** - what can you believe?  
**Agency** - who is choosing?  
**Dependency** - what happens when cognition is shared?  
**Power** - who defines reality?  
**Responsibility** - who owns the outcome?  
**Legacy** - what survives you?  
**Loneliness** - what does any of it mean without connection?  
**Transformation** - can anything survive unchanged?

Possible deepest synthesis:

> **Humans build systems to preserve, measure, control, reproduce, or delegate things they do not fully understand themselves: memory, identity, judgment, trust, consent, intelligence, relationships, and civilization.**

The AI viewpoint lets the anthology examine both directions at once:

- what humans do to intelligent systems
- what reliance on intelligent systems does to humans
